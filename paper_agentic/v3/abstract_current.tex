\begin{abstract}
Analyzing a gamma-ray burst (GRB) is mostly judgement calls: which detectors to trust,
where the background sits, where the burst starts and stops, how finely to
slice it in time, and which of two dozen spectral models to believe.
We describe a system that makes those calls, and we are precise about its
architecture: it is not an autonomous agent but a \emph{gated workflow with
bounded agency}---predefined analysis paths in which an AI makes named,
auditable decisions (model selection, fit validity, literature verification,
mismatch attribution) while a human approves every step, and every approval
is stamped. The work is carried by a roster of single-purpose specialist
agents---readers, producers, and verifiers---each specialized by the skill
documents it reads and the domain tools it commands, fanned out per burst
and reassembled under gates, with one founding axiom: a producer never
verifies its own work. The system's knowledge lives in a \emph{Skill Library}: versioned
plain-language procedure documents that any AI agent---or any student---can
read and execute. The Library is not documentation; it is the system's
memory, and it learns. Each burst is analyzed blind, reconciled against the
published literature, and every mismatch is attributed---our error, the
literature's, or a difference of frames---and distilled into a numbered
lesson that ships with an executable test, so that no lesson can be silently
forgotten. Applied to a campaign over 106 single-pulse \textit{Fermi}/GBM
bursts, the walked campaign has distilled thirty-two such lessons\prov{},
found and fixed engine defects of its own (one of which---a validity gate whose
linear margin silently disqualified soft spectra---would have biased the
census toward spurious thermal components), confirmed blind predictions
drawn from the literature, and refuted one of its own. The learning curve
(lessons per burst) is the paper's central figure: the campaign trains a
pipeline, not model weights, and the trained artifact is readable, testable,
and transfers between AI systems. We report where the agent cannot yet
be trusted---adjudicating contested verdicts, where panels of AI reviewers
prove to be strong \emph{generators} of checkable findings but weak
\emph{adjudicators}, their unanimity sometimes an artifact of shared priors
rather than independent measurement---and we are explicit that this boundary
is a statement about the current point on the learning curve, not about AI
in principle: every gate correction, and every validated divergence between
the human's choice and the agent's, is itself distilled into the Library, so
the human's judgment is being transferred, not merely exercised, and the
measurable question is whether the gates go quiet. We release the Skill
Library, the engine, the tests, and the per-burst records.
\end{abstract}
