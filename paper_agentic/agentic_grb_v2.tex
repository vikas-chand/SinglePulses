\begin{abstract}
Yes---with a human at the gate, and the distinction matters. Analyzing a
gamma-ray burst (GRB) is mostly judgement calls: which detectors to trust,
where the background sits, where the burst starts and stops, how finely to
slice it in time, and which of two dozen spectral models to believe.
We describe a system that makes those calls, and we are precise about its
architecture: it is not an autonomous agent but a \emph{gated workflow with
bounded agency}---predefined analysis paths in which an AI makes named,
auditable decisions (model selection, fit validity, literature verification,
mismatch attribution) while a human approves every step, and every approval
is stamped. The system's knowledge lives in a \emph{Skill Library}: versioned
plain-language procedure documents that any AI agent---or any student---can
read and execute. The Library is not documentation; it is the system's
memory, and it learns. Each burst is analyzed blind, reconciled against the
published literature, and every mismatch is attributed---our error, the
literature's, or a difference of frames---and distilled into a numbered
lesson that ships with an executable test, so that no lesson can be silently
forgotten. Applied to a campaign over 106 single-pulse \textit{Fermi}/GBM
bursts, the first ten walkthroughs\prov{} produced twenty-seven such lessons,
found and fixed five engine defects (one of which---a validity gate whose
linear margin silently disqualified soft spectra---would have biased the
census toward spurious thermal components), confirmed blind predictions
drawn from the literature, and refuted one of its own. The learning curve
(lessons per burst) is the paper's central figure: the campaign trains a
pipeline, not model weights, and the trained artifact is readable, testable,
and transfers between AI systems. We release the Skill Library, the engine,
the tests, and the per-burst records, and we report where the agent still
cannot be trusted: adjudicating scientifically contested verdicts, where we
show that panels of AI reviewers are strong \emph{generators} of checkable
findings but weak \emph{adjudicators}---their unanimity can be an artifact of
shared priors rather than independent measurement.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}
% ===========================================================================

The question in our title is usually asked as if it had a yes-or-no answer.
It does not, because ``AI doing analysis'' names at least two different
architectures, and the difference between them decides what the answer can
mean. In the first architecture, a \emph{workflow}, the analysis follows
predefined paths---the same stages, in the same order, for every burst---and
software executes them. In the second, an \emph{agent}, a model directs its
own process: it decides what to do next, which tools to call, and when it is
finished \TODOcite{Anthropic 2024 workflow/agent distinction; verify against
the published engineering post}. Workflows are auditable and dull; agents are
flexible and hard to trust. Most claims that ``AI analyzed the data'' blur
the two.

This paper builds and tests a deliberate hybrid, and names it honestly: a
\emph{gated workflow with bounded agency}. The analysis pipeline---data
acquisition, detector and background selection, adaptive binning, spectral
fitting, temporal analysis, quality control---is a workflow: its stages are
fixed, its statistics are chosen in advance, and its outputs are stamped
with provenance. Inside that workflow, an AI exercises agency at exactly the
points where judgement is required and we can afford to audit it: which of
twenty-four spectral models wins a gated comparison, whether a fit is valid
or sits on a parameter rail, whether a published value has been verified at
its source, and---when our numbers disagree with the literature---whether the
fault is ours, theirs, or a difference of analysis frames. A human approves
every step before the next one runs. Nothing advances on silence, and no
approval is ever fabricated: every decision carries a stamp naming who, or
what, made it.

Why is time-resolved GRB spectroscopy the right testbed rather than a
convenient one? Because it concentrates, in one measurement problem, the
three features that make scientific AI hard to evaluate. First, the
decisions have no answer key. Two careful published analyses of the same
burst, using overlapping data and defensible methods, can disagree about
whether a thermal component exists at all; the literature is a
\emph{distribution} over defensible choices, not a ground truth
\TODOcite{Siddique et al. 2022; Dirirsa et al. 2019 -- the same-burst
disagreement documented in our Burst Records}. Second, the instrument
matters: responses age, detectors are occulted, backgrounds are fit, and a
systematic that is invisible in one regime of burst brightness or hardness
appears only when the sample happens to present the other. Third, the
stakes are population-level: our science application is a census of
spectral shapes across 106 single-pulse bursts selected morphologically
\TODOcite{Busby \& Lazzati}, and a small per-burst bias becomes a
population-level artifact. A system that merely \emph{runs} on such data is
easy; a system whose outputs deserve trust must show its work at every one
of these pressure points.

Our design answer, developed in \S\ref{sec:workflow}, has one unusual
component that we believe is the paper's main methodological contribution.
The system's procedural knowledge lives in a \emph{Skill Library}: versioned,
plain-language documents---one per pipeline stage---that state how the stage
is run, what its known failure modes are, and what was learned from every
burst that ever taught it something. The Library is executable culture: an
AI agent reads the same document a new student would, and both are bound by
the same rules. Crucially, the Library \emph{learns} under a discipline we
call lessons-as-tests: a lesson enters the Library only as a claim with
provenance (which burst, which date, which evidence) \emph{and} an
executable test that fails if the lesson is ever violated again. Weights
are never updated; the harness is. \S\ref{sec:learning} shows what this
training regime produced over the first ten bursts of the campaign,
including its learning curve, and \S\ref{sec:verify} develops the
verification doctrine---blind-first analysis, frozen predictions, and an
independence requirement we state as: agreement between two analyses is
evidence only in proportion to how deep their shared primitives go.

We are not the first to ask whether AI can do science end-to-end.
Multi-agent systems now generate ideas, run analyses, and draft papers
across disciplines \TODOcite{Denario / Villaescusa-Navarro et al. 2025;
AI-Scientist v2; verify all}. Our design differs in direction: where those
systems are breadth-first---an agent writes fresh analysis code for each
project---ours is depth-first: the agents operate a single, tested,
domain-specific engine, and the system's growth is the accumulation of
verified domain lessons rather than of generated artifacts. The comparison
is instructive in both directions, and we return to it in
\S\ref{sec:discussion}: the breadth-first systems demonstrate, at scale,
exactly the evaluation gap---qualitative review standing in for ground
truth---that our gated, decision-level scoring is designed to close.

The paper is organized as follows. \S\ref{sec:sample} describes the sample
and data. \S\ref{sec:workflow} presents the workflow: the Campaign
Protocol, the Skill Library, the authority split between workflow, agent,
and human, and the doctrine distilled from its failures.
\S\ref{sec:learning} presents harness learning and the campaign learning
curve. \S\ref{sec:verify} presents the verification doctrine and the
adversarial experiments. \S\ref{sec:design} describes the human-versus-agent
benchmark design. \S\ref{sec:results} reports walkthrough-era results, all
provisional\prov{} until a frozen production sweep. \S\ref{sec:discussion}
discusses limits, and \S\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes. Throughout,
walkthrough-era numbers carry the flag\prov{}; they will be regenerated
mechanically when the converged pipeline is frozen and run once over the
full sample.

\subsection{The campaign loop} \label{sec:loop}

The unit of work is one burst, walked through eleven gated steps
(Fig.~\ref{fig:loop}). The \emph{Identity \& Circulars} step resolves which
burst this actually is---same-day triggers exist, and we show below that this
step has already intercepted a wrong redshift---and reads every real-time
circular. The \emph{Literature Harvest} step finds and fetches every refereed
paper that analyzed the burst, using four query forms (name, trigger name,
day-fraction, trigger number), preferring the version of record, and
recording each harvested value together with its conventions: which peak
energy definition, which sign convention, which energy band, which time
interval on whose clock, which detectors. The \emph{Data Inventory} step
verifies that the downloaded data can support the analysis: file versions,
response-matrix time coverage, and detector geometry---including checks that
no geometrically well-placed detector is physically occulted, a failure mode
invisible to pointing angles. The \emph{Selection} steps (detectors,
background intervals, source interval) are made by a human in a purpose-built
interface, or by a vision-capable agent, and in either case are recorded with
an approval stamp naming the decision-maker and time; the pipeline treats
these stamps as immutable inputs. The \emph{Adaptive Binning} step derives
Bayesian-block time bins merged to a significance floor. The \emph{Spectral
Fitting} step---where most of the agency lives---is described in
\S\ref{sec:fitstep}. \emph{Temporal analysis}, \emph{spectral-energy
panels}, and \emph{quality control} complete the ledger; the QC step returns
one of a set of typed terminal states, of which \texttt{non-identifiable} is
a \emph{successful} outcome: the workflow is forbidden to keep adjusting an
analysis until it manufactures a preference.

Two disciplines wrap the loop. Before any comparison with the literature, the
agent's own results are frozen---written to the Burst Record in a form that
cannot be revised---together with predictions derived from the harvested
literature (\S\ref{sec:verify}). Only then are the two compared, and every
mismatch is pushed to one of three attributions: \emph{we-wrong} (an error on
our side, which must be fixed and tested), \emph{they-wrong} (a documented
problem in the published analysis, stated with evidence), or
\emph{frame-difference} (both analyses defensible; the difference traced to
conventions, data selections, or eras, and recorded as a normalization rule).
The attribution, not the mismatch, is the product: it becomes a numbered
lesson in the Skill Library, shipped with an executable test
(\S\ref{sec:learning}).

The loop's exit condition is convergence: when $K$ consecutive bursts
complete without producing a single new lesson, the Skill Library is declared
converged, the pipeline is frozen at a recorded commit, and the entire
106-burst sample is re-analyzed once, from scratch, by the frozen system.
Only that production sweep produces citable numbers; every value in this
paper from the campaign era carries the provisional flag\prov. The design
premise, stated plainly: \emph{the per-burst numbers are not the product;
the matured Skill Library is.}

\subsection{Inside the fitting step} \label{sec:fitstep}

The fitting engine confronts each time bin with twenty-four spectral models:
four one-break continua, five carrying extra low-energy structure (thermal
or a second break), and fifteen high-energy composites. Three rules govern
what the numbers may mean. First, \emph{inclusion is gated on data quality,
never on detection significance}: every detector band with usable data and a
valid response enters the joint fit, because conditioning inclusion on
significance manufactures spurious detections in exactly the bursts where a
band fluctuated upward. Second, \emph{every model family receives
unconditional multistarts}, because a chained seed from a bright epoch can
strand a faint bin in a wrong local minimum---a failure we document in
\S\ref{sec:learning}. Third, \emph{validity gates} exclude from model
selection any fit whose shape parameters sit on a bound, with the margins
computed in the parameter's natural geometry (logarithmic for
energy-like parameters spanning decades)---a rule the campaign itself had to
learn, at measurable cost, from its first soft burst.

Model comparison is then reported as evidence, not verdicts: the winning
model's margin is quoted both against the runner-up (\emph{which} model) and
against the best one-break continuum (\emph{is extra structure needed at
all}), each converted to an evidence ratio $e^{\Delta\mathrm{AIC}/2}$ and
graded. A margin of 6 corresponds to roughly 20:1 and is called strong;
10, roughly 150:1, decisive; anything less is reported as what it is. Where
extra structure is required but thermal and non-thermal descriptions sit
within a few units of each other, the bin is assigned to a degeneracy class
rather than a physics claim: \emph{adequacy and identity are different
questions}, and the second frequently cannot be answered from these data
alone.

\subsection{Who decides what} \label{sec:authority}

Table~\ref{tab:authority} states the paper's honesty contract. The
\emph{workflow} decides everything decided in advance: the step order, the
energy selections, the statistic, the multistart seeds, the stamping of
provenance. The \emph{AI} decides, within audited bounds: which model wins
the gated comparison, whether a fit is valid, whether a published value has
been verified at source and in which frame, how a mismatch is attributed,
and which anomalies deserve escalation. The \emph{human} decides everything
consequential: the scope, every step gate, whether a proposed lesson enters
the Library, when convergence is declared, and every scientific claim that
leaves the system. In ten bursts\prov{} of operation, no approval was ever
generated by the system on the human's behalf; the reverse---the human
overruling the agent's proposal---occurred repeatedly and is part of the
record (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Two planes} \label{sec:planes}

Everything above describes the \emph{production plane}. A second,
\emph{discovery plane} exists for ideas that do not belong inside a frozen
workflow: anomalies that suggest new observables, hypotheses formed after
seeing data. Work there is sandboxed and carries no evidentiary weight until
it passes a promotion gate---calibration on simulations, validation on data
that did not motivate it, human approval. The governing rule is stated once
and enforced always: \emph{the agent may invent a new scorecard after seeing
the game; it may not pretend the scorecard was chosen before the game.}

\subsection{The doctrine} \label{sec:doctrine}

Seven rules, each purchased by a documented failure, govern all of the
above: (1) analyze blind first---published values are harvested before
fitting but the fit is never steered toward them; (2) margins are computed
over valid fits only; (3) evidence ratios, never bare verdicts; (4)
inclusion by data quality, never significance---and its absolute corollary:
production fits never drop data that passes the quality gate, with/without
refits are attribution diagnostics whose data-dropped member can never be
quoted as a result, and omission of data is legal only through a documented
quality exclusion; (5) a lesson is not learned
until it is a claim \emph{and} a test; (6) no silent approvals---a skipped
check is a fake pass; (7) verify claims at their source, and attribute
agreement or disagreement at the level of shared primitives. The failures
that funded each rule appear in \S\ref{sec:learning} and
\S\ref{sec:discussion}.
